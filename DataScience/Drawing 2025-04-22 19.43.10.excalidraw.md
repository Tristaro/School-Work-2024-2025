Mean: Average value of a dataset. ^0MNlHZnX

Mode: Most frequently occurring value. ^7ThL0BI9

Distribution Shapes ^hKCfCrR6

Symmetric: Left and right sides mirror each other. ^xLvkt2tS

Skewed Left: Tail on the left (e.g., exam scores where most students score high but a few score very low). ^YdnDo421

Skewed Right: Tail on the right (e.g., income distribution where most people earn lower amounts but a few earn very high). ^eCy8LZEb

Example: A histogram showing salaries where most employees earn $50,000 but a few executives earn $500,000 (right-skewed). ^mgg1ugXJ

Normal Distribution ^I0i0dMPi

Bell-shaped, symmetric curve where most values cluster around the mean. ^CqUusZmr

68-95-99.7 Rule: 68% of data falls within 1 standard deviation, 95% within 2, and 99.7% within 3. ^vUGMsRB0

Example: IQ scores follow a normal distribution with mean 100 and standard deviation 15. ^t0Tqi4ga

Z-score ^VO63IfK9

Measures how many standard deviations a value is from the mean. ^h8aBBe87

Formula: z = (x - μ) / σ ^YZPUbnF1

Example: If SAT scores have a mean of 500 and standard deviation of 100, a score of 650 has a z-score of (650-500)/100 = 1.5. ^eJrANH2q

Correlation (r) ^scBrbTEM

Measures strength & direction of a linear relationship between two variables (-1 to 1). ^HMDEMa1k

Example: Height and weight have a strong positive correlation (~0.8), meaning taller people tend to weigh more. ^rFP6Uule

Regression Equation ^CZvY74ci

Describes the relationship between dependent (Y) and independent (X) variables. ^I1hmrist

Formula: y = a + bx ^pLCo96W5

Example: Predicting weight (Y) based on height (X), where y = 50 + 0.5x means for every 1 cm increase in height, weight increases by 0.5 kg. ^C9GVGWUS

Residuals ^k1XfHFGE

Difference between actual and predicted values in regression. ^KXULVHl7

Formula: e = y - ŷ ^gaMplPgm

Example: If actual weight is 80 kg and predicted is 78 kg, residual is 80 - 78 = 2. ^rYN5uE7u

Relative Risk (RR) ^AI7iswWT

Compares risk between two groups. ^JZlUSolg

Formula: RR = P(disease | exposed) / P(disease | unexposed) ^joYP0Vwk

Example: Suppose we are studying lung cancer risk in smokers and non-smokers. The data is: ^HZcFm5Gg

Now, calculate the Relative Risk: RR = (0.10/0.02) = 5 This means that smokers are 5 times more likely to develop lung cancer compared to non-smokers. ^k1cEIRUx

RR=1: No difference in risk between the two groups. ^3TuegJQd

RR>1: Higher risk in the exposed group (risk factor). ^rFxU4Ktg

RR<1: Lower risk in the exposed group (protective factor). ^pFMLEAHE

Odds Ratio (OR) ^SLuMCvkk

Compares odds of an event occurring in two groups. ^JatY0G5Q

Formula: OR = (p1 / (1 - p1)) / (p0 / (1 - p0)) ^ZFa2V6Ko

Where: ^JDBJ0PNY

p1​ = Probability of the event in the exposed group. ^2VxxKG1m

p0​ = Probability of the event in the unexposed group. ^YnnOpswg

1−p1​ and 1−p0​ are the probabilities of the event not occurring in the respective groups. ^wgOFjDjL

Example: If 20% of exposed people develop a disease while 5% of non-exposed do, OR = (0.2/0.8) / (0.05/0.95) = 4. ^BSa0O82m
