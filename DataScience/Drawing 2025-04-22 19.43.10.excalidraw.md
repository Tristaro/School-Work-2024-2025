## Text Elements
Key Concepts & Definitions ^1isGSvRc

Data Types ^YpVYpkLO

Categorical: Data that represents groups or categories (e.g., eye color, gender, zip codes). ^KESRj5Im

Quantitative: Numerical data that represents amounts or measurements (e.g., height, weight, test scores). ^DzkxoXHP

Example: A dataset with students' favorite colors (categorical) and their heights (quantitative). ^elJtDjxh

Descriptive Statistics ^lXWvNHUC

Mean: Average value of a dataset. ^ChHxxT9y

Median: Middle value when data is ordered. ^XZOtExOb

Mode: Most frequently occurring value. ^hhRkMCYS

Variance & Standard Deviation: Measure how spread out the data is. ^JoE7IDdZ

Example: Given test scores [70, 80, 85, 90, 95], the mean is 84, median is 85, and standard deviation shows how much scores deviate from the mean. ^shtJAURC

Distribution Shapes ^Zq5ns61A

Symmetric: Left and right sides mirror each other. ^Ho6eGnZX

Skewed Left: Tail on the left (e.g., exam scores where most students score high but a few score very low). ^lgF5pb2I

Skewed Right: Tail on the right (e.g., income distribution where most people earn lower amounts but a few earn very high). ^PVv72suY

Example: A histogram showing salaries where most employees earn $50,000 but a few executives earn $500,000 (right-skewed). ^1NSZNm1U

Boxplots & Five-Number Summary ^US8PeGsg

Five-Number Summary: Minimum, Q1 (25th percentile), Median (50th percentile), Q3 (75th percentile), Maximum. ^FgF4AIj6

Boxplot: A visual representation of the five-number summary. ^FJfyTU6E

Example: A boxplot of student heights showing a median of 170 cm, with a few outliers above 190 cm. ^OzRjWudg

Normal Distribution ^NIjW0C85

Bell-shaped, symmetric curve where most values cluster around the mean. ^hDXvPLVq

68-95-99.7 Rule: 68% of data falls within 1 standard deviation, 95% within 2, and 99.7% within 3. ^vhd8BCsR

Example: IQ scores follow a normal distribution with mean 100 and standard deviation 15. ^MSC35Jwd

Z-score ^nKC5xvgU

Measures how many standard deviations a value is from the mean. ^cR0DfHeJ

Formula: z = (x - μ) / σ ^ncPt9N13

Example: If SAT scores have a mean of 500 and standard deviation of 100, a score of 650 has a z-score of (650-500)/100 = 1.5. ^gZCINkLg

Correlation (r) ^hVSDG1KV

Measures strength & direction of a linear relationship between two variables (-1 to 1). ^QGvt6GmB

Example: Height and weight have a strong positive correlation (~0.8), meaning taller people tend to weigh more. ^4k8BmFj6

Regression Equation ^y7Q9aza8

Describes the relationship between dependent (Y) and independent (X) variables. ^ryOvNPGS

Formula: y = a + bx ^43sCpeUl

Example: Predicting weight (Y) based on height (X), where y = 50 + 0.5x means for every 1 cm increase in height, weight increases by 0.5 kg. ^suzB4NhE

Residuals ^YXXcQ68m

Difference between actual and predicted values in regression. ^eEIsPhg1

Formula: e = y - ŷ ^xJOyy5cD

Example: If actual weight is 80 kg and predicted is 78 kg, residual is 80 - 78 = 2. ^zW1H2zeL

Relative Risk (RR) ^BRYodJlg

Compares risk between two groups. ^sGMyY04r

Formula: RR = P(disease | exposed) / P(disease | unexposed) ^M2qgLo3l

Example: Suppose we are studying lung cancer risk in smokers and non-smokers. The data is: ^GRuFJGqM

Group ^qlBUD7Js

People with Lung Cancer ^KgUW3i3n

Total People ^hqA20Z2O

Probability (PPP) ^8WHpeZe9

Smokers ^hfucEBSr

10 ^PK1KR08X

100 ^gvd0oHjn

10/100=0.10 ^ZYGyRFgz

Non-Smokers ^gYZbQx0n

2 ^r8iwhZz3

100 ^aBuHKTcb

2/100=0.02 ^mDwRGqyZ

Now, calculate the Relative Risk: RR = (0.10/0.02) = 5 This means that smokers are 5 times more likely to develop lung cancer compared to non-smokers. ^YrOUTS2f

RR=1: No difference in risk between the two groups. ^SrDtLxNK

RR>1: Higher risk in the exposed group (risk factor). ^s43vxDAQ

RR<1: Lower risk in the exposed group (protective factor). ^sqMplJto

Odds Ratio (OR) ^UHnvLNOq

Compares odds of an event occurring in two groups. ^D0tdkC4A

Formula: OR = (p1 / (1 - p1)) / (p0 / (1 - p0)) ^36sTPtn1

Where: ^Jx40NvlH

p1​ = Probability of the event in the exposed group. ^sc84zkVJ

p0​ = Probability of the event in the unexposed group. ^T05Jx0WJ

1−p1​ and 1−p0​ are the probabilities of the event not occurring in the respective groups. ^Ng0Exnc2

Example: If 20% of exposed people develop a disease while 5% of non-exposed do, OR = (0.2/0.8) / (0.05/0.95) = 4. ^PYUUNDHO
