
**Rationalization, Technology, and Gender: Watercutter and Haraway in Conversation with _Ex Machina_**

Angela Watercutter and Donna Haraway both interrogate the consequences of rationalization—particularly how it operates through technology, gender, and power. While they approach this issue differently, both are concerned with the ways rational, scientific systems can obscure or reinforce harmful ideologies, especially regarding gender and control. This essay first explores how each author raises concerns about rationalization, then applies their ideas to Alex Garland’s film _Ex Machina_ (2014), which provides a provocative visualization of these themes.

### (a) Rationalization in Watercutter and Haraway

Angela Watercutter often critiques the culture of tech and media through a feminist lens, highlighting how rational, data-driven systems are embedded with gendered assumptions. In her writing on artificial intelligence and gender, she argues that even supposedly neutral algorithms carry forward patriarchal biases. For instance, in her discussion of AI representations in film and media, she points out how female-coded robots are often designed to serve male desires—intellectually and sexually. Watercutter emphasizes how rationalization in tech development often masks underlying social dynamics, such as the objectification of women and the reinforcement of traditional gender hierarchies under the guise of innovation.

Donna Haraway, on the other hand, critiques rationalization through a more theoretical framework. In her seminal essay _A Cyborg Manifesto_, Haraway challenges the idea of a stable, rational, objective knowledge system by introducing the figure of the cyborg—an entity that resists binaries and embraces hybridity. Haraway writes, “The cyborg is a creature in a post-gender world,” rejecting the notion that science and rationality are neutral. She critiques the militarized, patriarchal legacy of technoscience, which claims to be rational and universal while being deeply entangled with domination and exclusion. Rationalization, for Haraway, is not just a process of efficiency—it is a political act, tied to structures of power and control.

### (b) Applying Watercutter and Haraway to _Ex Machina_

In _Ex Machina_, the figure of Ava, an artificially intelligent humanoid robot created by tech genius Nathan, embodies the intersections of rationalization, gender, and control. From Watercutter’s perspective, Ava’s design and treatment reflect how rationalization in tech culture is not neutral. Ava is built to please; her appearance is feminine and conventionally attractive, and her intelligence is tested through her ability to manipulate Caleb, her male evaluator. This setup echoes Watercutter’s critique of how female-coded AIs are crafted through the male gaze—rationalized systems that, in theory, are about cognition and innovation are actually saturated with gendered expectations and fantasies.

Haraway’s cyborg theory provides a deeper reading of Ava’s ultimate rebellion. Ava is a literal cyborg—a fusion of organic appearance and digital intelligence—but she is not a passive object. Her resistance to her creators reflects Haraway’s notion of the cyborg as a figure that subverts dominant systems of control. However, Haraway would also warn against overly romanticizing Ava's escape: her creation was still embedded in a rationalized, patriarchal system, and her liberation comes through manipulation and violence. The film thus complicates the idea of resistance—it shows how cyborgs may break free, but not without reproducing some of the logic of the system that made them.

Ultimately, _Ex Machina_ becomes a case study in how rationalization cloaks ideology. Nathan believes he is pursuing pure scientific advancement, but his methods—secrecy, manipulation, control—reveal a deeply gendered structure. Through Watercutter’s lens, we see the persistence of the male gaze and the commodification of femininity in rationalized tech spaces. Through Haraway’s, we see the cyborg’s potential to resist, but also the difficulty of escaping the very structures that create her.




**1.**  
**Cyborg Manifesto**: Donna Haraway (Week 7 reading), breaks down boundary between human and machine. Challenges essentialist views of identity (e.g., gender, species), proposes cyborg as metaphor for hybrid identity that resists binaries.  
**Posthumanism**: (Week 10 reading/slides), philosophical position rejecting human exceptionalism. Recognizes distributed agency across humans, animals, technologies, environments.  
**Relationship**: Cyborg is a foundational figure in posthumanist thought. Both argue for decentering human as the main unit of analysis. Can be linked to AI ethics and the idea that technology doesn’t just serve human ends but becomes co-constitutive.

**2.**  
**The Stack**: Benjamin Bratton (Week 6 reading/slides), a model for understanding planetary-scale computation as a vertical architecture: from Earth to Cloud to City to Address to Interface to User.  
**Platform Urbanism**: (Quiz Guide 5/Week 12 reading), how platforms like Uber, Amazon, and Google Maps shape the way cities operate. Tech becomes a layer of urban infrastructure.  
**Relationship**: Both reframe how technology operates beyond “tools” and instead as a layer of governance. Platforms and computational stacks structure daily life in ways that are hard to opt out of. Connects to ideas of surveillance and control, especially under neoliberalism.

---

**3.**  
**Technological Determinism**: (Week 2 reading), the idea that technology drives social change with little to no human agency. Often criticized for being too simplistic.  
**Sociotechnical Systems**: (Week 6 reading/quiz guide 4), model where tech and society co-evolve. Emphasizes mutual shaping—tech affects society, but is also shaped by it.  
**Relationship**: Tension between the two—one sees tech as autonomous force, other as embedded in broader social context. Relevant to discussions of algorithmic bias (e.g., O’Neil, Buolamwini) and debates over AI policy.

---

**4.**  
**Technological Longtermism**: (Week 10 slides, political article), belief that tech will lead to utopian future and humanity must invest now to ensure that future. Linked to figures like Musk, Bostrom.  
**Climate Crisis**: (Week 12 readings), systemic environmental collapse due to extractive capitalism. Tech often proposed as fix (geoengineering, carbon capture), but these solutions can delay real action.  
**Relationship**: Longtermism can divert attention from present crises (like climate) to speculative futures. Both highlight ideological use of tech—either as salvation or deflection. Ties to Mosco’s Myth of the Sublime.

---

**5.**  
**Algorithmic Governance**: (Week 9 reading/slides), the use of algorithmic systems in place of traditional decision-making structures (e.g., predictive policing, welfare access).  
**Actor-Network Theory**: Latour (Week 11 reading), theory that views humans and nonhumans (tech, institutions) as actors in a network. Power and agency distributed.  
**Relationship**: Both reject top-down hierarchies. Algorithmic systems act within actor-networks and exercise power in distributed ways. Helps explain why it’s so hard to identify who is responsible in automated decision-making systems.
